## Step One: Deciding Between a Short Plat and a Long Plat
Before you start drawing lines on a map, you need to know which regulatory path you are on. In Klickitat County, the "number of lots" is the primary factor, but it's not the only one.

### 1. The Short Plat (Up to 4 Lots)
This is the most common path for private landowners.
*   **Scope:** Creating 4 or fewer lots.
*   **The 5-Year Rule:** You can only Short Plat the same parent parcel once every 5 years.
*   **Review Body:** Handled by the Planning Director (faster, no public hearing).
*   **Requirements:** Environmental Review, Soil Application, Road access requirement, Survey Required. Less stringent utility and infrastructure requirements than a full subdivion. 
*   **Official Doc:** [Short Plat Requirements](https://www.klickitatcounty.gov/DocumentCenter/View/13656/SHORT-PLAT)

### 2. The Long Plat (5+ Lots)
This is a full-scale subdivision process.
*   **Scope:** Creating 5 or more lots 
*   **The 5-year Rule:** You will have to long-plat for 4 or fewer lots if it is within 5 years.
*   **Review Body:** Subject to public hearings and approval by the Planning Commission and Board.
*   **Requirements:** Environmental Review, Soil Application, Road access requirement, Survey Required. More stringent utility and infrastructure requirements than a short plat. 
*   **Official Doc:** [Subdivision Requirements](https://www.klickitatcounty.gov/DocumentCenter/View/13657/SUBDIVISION)

## Step Two: The Pre-submission Meeting
Once you've decided on your path, your next move is to meet with the county and gather all of the requirements for your project. You do not need to hire an engineer or surveyor at this point. 
*   **Action:** Reach out to the [Klickitat County Planning Department](https://www.klickitatcounty.gov/Directory.aspx?DID=17) to schedule a pre-submission meeting.
*   **Requirement:** You only need a drawing of your proposed land division. A formal survey is **not** required at this stage.
*   **Goal:** Use this meeting to find out exactly what the county will require to approve your project.

## My Current Project
We ran into a couple of surprises at our pre-submission meeting.

First, the county warned us that the State might require an archaeological review of the land for Native American artifacts. They noted it’s unlikely since the land was recently short-platted and approved, but it’s not impossible.

Second, the county might force us to install a fire hydrant. Fire hydrants are typically not required when creating less than 5 additional lots, but because we are using the formal long plat subdivision process, they can enforce the regulation.

Given the increasing wildfire risk in our area, I am not totally opposed to installing a fire hydrant. However, this will significantly impact our water infrastructure budget. 

Here is the math: Standard residential homes use a ¾-inch water connection. To service our three new homes, we could comfortably run a 4-inch or 6-inch line. But to support a fire hydrant, we are required to pull an 8-inch water main off the existing 6-inch main line.

This is a prime example of why land development is never just about drawing lines on a map. It requires anticipating the infrastructure demands buried deep in the county code. We are meeting with the city and our engineer on Friday to review these requirements and see if we can find a more cost-effective solution that still mitigates the fire risk.
