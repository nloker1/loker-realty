# Decoding White Salmon's R2 Zone

If you read the White Salmon Municipal Code (WSMC) for the R2 High-Density zone, you might think the math is simple. **WSMC 17.28.040.A** explicitly states: *“Maximum number of primary dwelling structures per lot: Four.”*

To a developer or a homeowner, that sounds like a green light to build four small, detached cottages on a single lot. But if you try to pull a permit for that today, you’ll likely be told "No."

### The Conflict: Structures vs. Buildings

The debate boils down to a head-on collision between two parts of the code:

1. **The Density Provision (The "Four" Rule):** The code says you can have four "primary dwelling structures." A primary structure is defined as any home that isn't an ADU.
2. **The Use Definition (The "Multiplex" Rule):** However, the code says the *way* you get those units is through a "Multiplex." And here is the catch: **WSMC 17.08.430** defines a multiplex as **“a building”** (singular) arranged for more than two families.

### The Hearing Examiner’s Final Word

This isn't just a theoretical debate. It was recently tested when a property owner tried to build a second primary home on an R2 lot. The City denied it, and the Hearing Examiner upheld that denial.

The Examiner’s logic was that to make the whole code work together, the "Four" in the density section must refer to **units within a single building**, not four separate buildings.

**The Current Reality:** 

* **Allowed:** One single-family home (with up to two ADUs).
* **Allowed:** One single "Multiplex" building containing up to four units. 
* **NOT Allowed:** Four separate, detached cottages.

### The "Breezeway" Battle

Developers have tried to find a middle ground by connecting separate homes with breezeways or carports to call them "one building." However, the Planning Commission has signaled that a simple breezeway isn't enough to turn two duplexes into one "multiplex."

To be legal in the eyes of the City right now, the units must be **structurally attached.** This means the code is effectively forcing "big box" architecture—one large building—instead of the "cottage cluster" style that many feel fits our small-town character better.

### The "Intensity" Question: How Much is Too Much?

Allowing four separate primary homes on a single city lot—which can be as small as 5,000 square feet in the R2 zone—is an intense level of density.

To some, this sounds like a "density nightmare" that could overcrowd our streets. To others, it sounds like the only way to create truly affordable "missing middle" housing for local workers and young families who want to own their own home rather than rent an apartment.
