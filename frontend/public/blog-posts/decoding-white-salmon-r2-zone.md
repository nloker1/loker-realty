## What is Permitted?

If you read the White Salmon Municipal Code (WSMC) for the R2 High-Density zone, you might think the math is simple. WSMC 17.28.040.A explicitly states: *“Maximum number of primary dwelling structures per lot: Four.”*

To a developer or a homeowner, that sounds like a green light to build four small, detached cottages on a single lot. But if you try to pull a permit for that today, you’ll likely be told "No."

## The Planning Commission’s Interpretation

The Planning Commission, acting on city staff recommendations, has interpreted the R2 density provisions to mean that primary dwelling structures must be contained within **one single structure** and cannot be detached, separate buildings.

The basis for this restriction comes from a collision with a completely different section of the code. WSMC 17.08.430 defines a multiplex as *“a building”* (singular) arranged for more than two families. Because the code uses "structures" (plural) in the density section but "building" (singular) in the definition section, the language is highly ambiguous and open to conflicting interpretations.



## The Hearing Examiner’s Final Word

This isn't just a theoretical debate; it was recently tested in the real world. When a property owner tried to build a second primary home on an R2 lot, the City denied the permit, and the Hearing Examiner upheld that denial.

The Examiner’s logic was that to make the entire code work together consistently, the "Four" in the density section must refer to **individual units within a single building**, not four separate, detached buildings.

## The Current Reality

As the rules are enforced right now, here is the baseline for what you can and cannot build on an R2 lot:

- **Allowed:** One single-family home (with up to two ADUs).
- **Allowed:** One single "Multiplex" building containing up to four attached units.
- **NOT Allowed:** Four separate, detached cottages.

## The Great Density Debate

This technical nuance highlights a much larger conversation happening across our community. Many White Salmon residents have voiced displeasure, arguing that the current R2 code allows for too much density and threatens to destroy our small-town feel. On the other side of the coin, the local need for affordable, attainable housing is deeply real.

Finding a functional balance between preserving community character and opening doors for affordable housing options remains one of the toughest challenges facing White Salmon today.
